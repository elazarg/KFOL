
\section{Complexity of Forceability}
\label{sec:k-complexity}

We analyze the complexity of the forceability problem:
given a finite structure $\mathcal M$, a closed \KFOL\ formula $\Phi$,
and a player $i$, decide if $F_i(\Phi,\mathcal M)$ is true.
We assume $\mathcal M$ is fixed and the formula $\Phi$ is the input.

\subsection{The Broadcast Core is PSPACE-complete}

First, we observe that the core calculus (without $\setminus V$ binders)
is PSPACE-complete, by a standard reduction from Quantified
Boolean Formulas (QBF).

\begin{proposition}[PSPACE-completeness of Core]
\label[proposition]{prop:pspace-core}
For fixed $K \ge 2$, the forceability problem $F_i$
for the broadcast fragment (no $\setminus V$ binders)
over finite structures is PSPACE-complete.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Membership in PSPACE is by a standard alternating Turing machine.
We can evaluate $F_i(\Phi, \mathcal{M})$ by a recursive algorithm
that simulates the game.
For a binder $P_i x.\,\Phi'$, it existentially guesses $a \in |\mathcal{M}|$
and recurses. For $P_j x.\,\Phi'$ (where $j \ne i$), it
universally checks all $a \in |\mathcal{M}|$ and recurses.
Since the domain $|\mathcal{M}|$ is finite, this takes polynomial
space in the depth of the formula.

Hardness is by reduction from QBF. Let $\psi$ be a QBF
\[
  Q_1 x_1 \dots Q_n x_n.\; f(x_1, \dots, x_n)
\]
over Boolean variables. We build a $K=2$ formula $\Phi_\psi$
over a structure $\mathcal{M}$ with domain $\{0, 1\}$.
This is a \textbf{one-hot} (\textbf{zero-sum} or \textbf{conflicting}) game,
as defined in \Cref{sec:outcome-classes}.
\[
\Phi_\psi :=
  P_{q(1)} x_1.\;
  P_{q(2)} x_2.\; \dots\;
  P_{q(n)} x_n.\;
  \langle f, \neg f \rangle
\]
where $q(j) = 0$ if $Q_j = \exists$ and $q(j) = 1$ if $Q_j = \forall$.
(Assuming $K=2$; for $K>2$ we pad with $\top$).
The QBF $\psi$ is true if and only if player $P_0$ can
force their objective, i.e., $F_0(\Phi_\psi, \mathcal{M})$ holds.
Since QBF is PSPACE-complete, so is our problem.
\end{proof}

\subsection{IF-style Independence is NEXPTIME-hard}

When we add the IF-style binders, the complexity jumps.
The problem is now at least as hard as Dependency QBF (DQBF),
which is NEXPTIME-complete. This construction needs variable-level
independence (each existential binder excluding an arbitrary,
binder-specific subset of prior variables); \Cref{subsec:if-binders} makes
this the primitive form of $\setminus V$ for exactly this reason.

\begin{proposition}[NEXPTIME-hardness with IF-binders]
\label[proposition]{prop:nexptime-if}
For fixed $K \ge 2$, the forceability problem $F_i$
with $\setminus V$ binders is NEXPTIME-hard.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
We reduce from DQBF. A DQBF instance consists of a formula
\[
  \forall y_1 \dots \forall y_n \; \exists x_1 \dots \exists x_m.\; f(\vec{y}, \vec{x})
\]
and for each $x_j$, a dependency set $D_j \subseteq \{y_1, \dots, y_n\}$.
The instance is true iff there exist Skolem functions $f_1, \dots, f_m$,
where each $f_j$ \emph{only} depends on the variables in $D_j$,
such that $\forall \vec{y}, f(\vec{y}, f_1(D_1), \dots, f_m(D_m))$ holds.

We build a $K=2$ formula $\Phi$ over $\mathcal{M}=\{0,1\}$.
Let $P_1$ be the $\forall$ player and $P_0$ be the $\exists$ player.
For each $x_j$, define its ``hidden set'' $H_j$ as the
variables $x_j$ is \emph{not} allowed to see:
$H_j := \{y_1, \dots, y_n\} \setminus D_j$.

The formula $\Phi$ is:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Phi :=
  P_1 y_1.\; \dots\; P_1 y_n.\;
  & P_0 x_1 \setminus H_1.\; \\
  & P_0 x_2 \setminus H_2.\; \\
  & \dots \\
  & P_0 x_m \setminus H_m.\;
  \langle f, \neg f \rangle
\end{aligned}
\]
The DQBF instance is true if and only if $F_0(\Phi, \mathcal{M})$ holds.

($\Rightarrow$) If DQBF is true, the Skolem functions $f_j$ exist.
$P_0$'s strategy $\profile_0$ is defined as $\profile_0(P_0 x_j \setminus H_j, s) := f_j(s|_{D_j})$.
This strategy is valid because it is independent of $H_j$.
By construction, it forces $f$ to be true.

($\Leftarrow$) If $F_0(\Phi, \mathcal{M})$ holds, then a valid strategy
$\profile_0$ exists. The binder $P_0 x_j \setminus H_j$
\emph{forces} $\profile_0$ to be independent of $H_j$.
This means $\profile_0$'s choice for $x_j$ can only depend on
variables \emph{not} in $H_j$, which is exactly $D_j$.
This strategy $\profile_0$ \emph{is} the set of
Skolem functions $f_j(D_j)$ that witnesses the DQBF.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[This construction is a $K=2$, single-role phenomenon]
\label{rem:nexptime-is-k2}
The hardness in \Cref{prop:nexptime-if} already appears at $K=2$, and every
$x_j$ on the existential side is bound by the \emph{same} role $P_0$: the
independence driving the DQBF reduction is between binders owned by \emph{one}
role ($P_0 x_j \setminus H_j$ for varying $j$), not between distinct roles or
coalitions of roles. So this result shows that a single role can fail to act
as one self-visible agent once its own moves are mutually restricted; it does
not by itself show that $K \ge 3$ roles, or coalitions of roles, do anything a
two-role game cannot already do. We return to this distinction below, where it
turns out to matter for how to read the next subsection.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Coalitional Forceability}
\label{subsec:coalitional-forceability}

Having $K > 2$ roles raises a natural further question: can a
\emph{coalition} of roles jointly force all of its members' objectives at
once, using strategies that may depend on each other's earlier moves however
the visibility structure allows?

\begin{definition}[Coalitional Forceability]
Let $S \subseteq \{0,\ldots,K-1\}$ be a coalition.
$S$ can jointly force its objectives if
\[
F_S(\Phi,\mathcal M)
:= \exists \{\profile_i\}_{i \in S}\;
   \forall \{\profile_j\}_{j \notin S}\;
   \bigwedge_{i \in S} \bigl( \llbracket \Phi \rrbracket_{\mathcal M, \profile} \bigr)_i = 1.
\]
\end{definition}

\paragraph{Relation to effectivity functions and Quantified Coalition Logic.}
The map $S \mapsto F_S(\Phi,\mathcal M)$ is an instance of an \emph{effectivity
function}~\cite{MoulinPeleg1982EffectivityFunctions}: a specification of which
outcomes each coalition can guarantee. Pauly's representation theorem shows
Coalition Logic is the modal logic of \emph{playable} effectivity
functions~\cite{pauly2002modal}, later corrected to \emph{truly} playable
functions to handle infinite outcome spaces~\cite{GorankoJamrogaTurrini2013TrulyPlayable}.
Closer still, quantifying over coalitions directly in the object language ---
``some coalition satisfying property $P$ can force $\phi$'' --- is already
Quantified Coalition Logic's central
operator~\cite{AgotnesVanDerHoekWooldridge2008QCL}. We are not aware of a
prior treatment of coalitional forceability specifically inside a
first-order, player-labeled quantifier-prefix calculus like \KFOL's, but the
coalition-quantification idea itself is not new, and any future extension of
\KFOL\ with an object-language coalition-quantifying operator should be
scoped against this literature rather than presented as novel on that count
alone.

A previous version of this section claimed $F_S$ was a hard problem ``not
present in 2-player logic,'' with a proof that in fact only ever instantiated
$S=\{0\}$: a singleton coalition, i.e.\ exactly the individual-forceability
problem of \Cref{prop:pspace-core} under a different name, padding every other
role with a vacuous objective $\top$. That argument says nothing about
coalitions with $|S|\ge 2$, the only case in which ``coalition'' means
anything beyond ``individual.'' We withdraw it. Using the coalition-forming
apparatus already defined in \Cref{def:coalitions}, the honest statement is
close to the opposite of what was claimed:

\begin{proposition}[Coalition merging is lossless under broadcast visibility]
\label{prop:coalition-merge-lossless}
Let $\Phi$ be a closed \KFOL\ formula with no $\setminus V$ binders (broadcast
fragment), let $S \subseteq \{0,\dots,K-1\}$, and let $\pi$ be the surjection
onto $\{0,1\}$ with $\pi^{-1}(0)=S$ and $\pi^{-1}(1) = \{0,\dots,K-1\}\setminus
S$. Then
\[
F_S(\Phi, \mathcal M) \iff F_0\bigl(\CoalProjAnd{\pi}(\Phi), \mathcal M\bigr).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \Cref{def:pure-strategy}, a strategy $\profile_i$ for role $i$ is already a
single function ranging over \emph{every} binder owned by $i$, keyed by the
history visible at that binder. Under broadcast visibility, relabeling a
binder from $P_i$ to $P_{\pi(i)}$ (as $\CoalForm{\pi}{\wedge}$ does) changes
neither the binder's position in the prefix nor the history visible at it: it
only renames which strategy fills it. Hence a family $\{\profile_i\}_{i\in
S}$ of per-role strategies and a single merged strategy for role $0$ in
$\CoalProjAnd{\pi}(\Phi)$ are the same underlying data, viewed either split by
original owner or joined into one function on the disjoint union of domains;
likewise for $\{\profile_j\}_{j\notin S}$ against role $1$. The terminal of
$\CoalProjAnd{\pi}(\Phi)$ has first component $\bigwedge_{i\in S}\fo_i$,
matching the conjunction in $F_S$ exactly, and $F_0$ inspects only that
component. The two sides therefore quantify over the same strategy spaces and
test the same condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Complexity of coalitional forceability, broadcast fragment]
\label{cor:coalitional-pspace}
For any fixed $K \ge 2$ and any $S \subseteq \{0,\dots,K-1\}$, $F_S$ without
$\setminus V$ binders is PSPACE-complete: both membership and hardness follow
from \Cref{prop:pspace-core} applied to the two-role formula
$\CoalProjAnd{\pi}(\Phi)$, via \Cref{prop:coalition-merge-lossless}. No new
reduction is needed.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Where, if anywhere, coalitions have separate content]
\Cref{prop:coalition-merge-lossless} is specific to the broadcast fragment,
and the proof shows exactly why: merging relies on relabeling not changing any
binder's visible history. This breaks as soon as $\setminus V$ binders are
present, because a slash-set can name a \emph{specific} role (``independent
of role $j$''), and after merging $S$ into one role that name either refers to
a member of the merged role itself or vanishes along with the role it named.
Concretely, if $\Phi$ has a binder $P_i x \setminus \{j\}.\,\Phi'$ with
$i,j\in S$, $i \ne j$ --- role $i$'s move must not depend on role $j$'s
earlier move, even though $i,j$ are coalition partners --- there is no
faithful way to fold this into a single merged strategy for $S$: dropping the
restriction overstates what the coalition can jointly achieve, and keeping it
means the ``merged'' role is not really merged.

This last claim needs a correction. Stated this bluntly it is too strong: a
$\setminus V$-crossing within a coalition does not, by itself, force a loss.
\Cref{prop:cross-diagonal-forceability} exhibits exactly this crossing pattern
inside a coalition (the cross-diagonal product $\circledast$, e.g.\
Battleship) and shows the merge is nonetheless lossless there, because the two
members' objectives are separable --- neither could have used the withheld
information anyway. What should matter is not the syntactic crossing but
whether it \emph{couples} the coalition's objectives: whether some counterfactual
strategy exploiting the withheld information would provably do better. We do
not have a general criterion for when that is the case, so the conjecture
below should be read as requiring coupled objectives, not merely a $\setminus
V$-crossing.

With that correction, this still suggests, but does not prove, where genuine
$K\ge 3$ content in this calculus would have to live: coalitions whose own
members are mutually information-restricted by IF-binders \emph{and} whose
objectives are coupled across that restriction. \Cref{prop:nexptime-if}
already shows that independence between binders owned by a \emph{single} role
breaks naive analysis (\Cref{rem:nexptime-is-k2}), but, as noted there, that
construction uses $K=2$ and one role's self-restriction, not restrictions
between distinct roles of a coalition with coupled goals. We do not have a
construction that separates coalitional forceability with IF-binders from its
two-coalition projections for $K\ge 3$. We conjecture one exists, along the
lines sketched above, and we pose it as the main open technical question
raised by this section (see also \Cref{sec:conclusion}). This conjecture is
stated information-theoretically throughout; whether it survives, strengthens,
or collapses once independence is realized by (and relaxed to) computational
cryptographic primitives rather than assumed exactly is a separate question we
have not addressed, posed precisely in
\Cref{subsec:computational-soundness-gap}.

One prerequisite of this conjecture is now checked rather than assumed: the
grammar must actually be able to express cross-role, per-binder information
restriction of the kind a construction along these lines would need.
\Cref{subsec:if-binders}'s correction to variable-level exclusion confirms it
can --- a binder owned by one role of a coalition can exclude an arbitrary
subset of prior variables, including ones bound by another role of the
\emph{same} coalition, which is exactly the pattern
\Cref{prop:cross-diagonal-forceability} shows is \emph{not} sufficient by
itself (coupling is still required) but which role-level exclusion could not
even have expressed. This does not make the conjecture true; it removes one
way it could have been vacuous.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[What a general coalition-quantifying operator would need, if built]
\label{rem:coalition-operator-parameters}
Suppose \KFOL\ were extended with an object-language operator quantifying
over coalitions, in the style of Quantified Coalition
Logic~\cite{AgotnesVanDerHoekWooldridge2008QCL}. Such an operator would have
(at least) four parameters: (i) a \emph{coalition family} $\mathcal S
\subseteq 2^{\{0,\dots,K-1\}}$ to quantify over --- note that a monotone
coalition family is itself a \emph{simple game} in Shapley's
sense~\cite{Shapley1962CompoundSimpleGames}, so this parameter connects
directly to voting-theoretic structure; (ii) a \emph{goal-aggregation} rule
per coalition (conjunctive, as $\CoalProjAnd{}$ already does, disjunctive, or
threshold); (iii) an \emph{environment attitude} --- demonic/forceability, or
one of the admissible/rational/secure alternatives of
\Cref{sec:framework}; and (iv) an \emph{information/merge policy} for how a
coalition's own members are allowed to share visibility, i.e.\ which lens
(\Cref{subsec:open-games}) realizes the merge. For fixed $K$, parameters (i)
and (ii) alone add no expressive power beyond a finite Boolean combination of
the $F_S$ primitives already in this section: an operator varying only those
two is syntactic sugar. Parameters (iii) and (iv) are exactly the two axes
this paper keeps finding to be load-bearing --- the solution-concept
spectrum of \Cref{sec:framework} and the lens-realizability question of
\Cref{subsec:open-games} --- so a general operator's real content, if it has
any, is concentrated there. We have not built this operator; this remark
records what its design space looks like so a future attempt is not mistaken
for new content when only (i) and (ii) are varied.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[An informal illustration: balance of power]
\label{rem:balance-of-power}
Classical balance-of-power theory offers a natural-language test of the
previous remark's distinction. Read \emph{statically} --- no coalition is
currently strong enough to profitably dominate the rest --- balance of power
is analyzed via the core or von Neumann--Morgenstern \emph{stable
sets}~\cite{VonNeumannMorgenstern1944}, and in ``might makes right'' settings
with no external enforcement the core is typically empty or degenerate,
which is the formal echo of such systems being dynamically deterred rather
than statically stable~\cite{Jordan2006PillageAndProperty}; the dedicated
international-relations formalization is
Niou--Ordeshook--Rose's~\cite{NiouOrdeshookRose1989BalanceOfPower}. Read this
way, balance of power is a Boolean property of the coalition landscape ---
$\forall S.\ \neg F_S(\text{hegemony})$ --- and by
\Cref{rem:coalition-operator-parameters} it is sugar: parameters (i)--(ii)
only, hence a two-coalition fact under this paper's flattening results. Read
\emph{dynamically} instead --- stability sustained because an attempted
power grab would trigger a \emph{responding} counter-coalition, the
mechanism of alliance politics --- the relevant concepts are \emph{bargaining
sets}~\cite{AumannMaschler1964BargainingSet} and \emph{farsighted
stability}~\cite{Chwe1994FarsightedCoalitionalStability,RayVohra2015FarsightedStableSet},
where the deterring response is chosen \emph{after}, and in reaction to,
information about the deviation --- parameter (iv): who learns what, when,
before reacting. We do not know whether the dynamic reading resists
flattening in \KFOL's sense; we note only that the literature itself needed
a strictly more complex concept to capture the same intuition once
information timing entered the picture, which is suggestive rather than
conclusive.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A third complexity regime: hierarchical independence]
\label{rem:hierarchical-if}
The two complexity results so far are extremes: no $\setminus V$ restriction
at all (\Cref{prop:pspace-core}, PSPACE-complete) and fully general,
possibly-incomparable $\setminus V$ restriction (\Cref{prop:nexptime-if},
NEXPTIME-hard via DQBF). Call a formula's $\setminus V$-annotations
\emph{hierarchical} if, for every two binders, the sets of earlier binders
each is permitted to see are $\subseteq$-comparable --- no two binders have
genuinely incomparable views of the prior history, only nested ones. This is
a purely syntactic, checkable condition, strictly weaker than ``no
restriction'' and strictly stronger than ``arbitrary $\setminus V$.''

Peterson and Reif's multiperson alternation
model~\cite{PetersonReif1979MultiplePersonAlternation}, and its refinement to
\emph{hierarchical} multiplayer games of incomplete information by Peterson,
Reif, and
Azhar~\cite{PetersonReifAzhar2001LowerBounds,PetersonReifAzhar2002DecisionAlgorithms},
is the direct precedent for this restriction: in their model, players whose
information forms a nested (clique) hierarchy of depth $d$ have a
\emph{decidable} outcome problem with a $(d{+}1)$-fold-exponential time
bound, strictly between the alternation-only case and the fully general
case, which they show can be \emph{undecidable} --- the same wall
distributed-synthesis hits for non-hierarchical
architectures~\cite{PnueliRosner1990DistributedHard}.

\emph{Three separable claims, of different strength.} We were imprecise
about this in an earlier draft of this remark, so we spell it out.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Is the general (non-hierarchical) IF fragment even
  decidable?} \Cref{prop:nexptime-if} proves only a lower bound (hardness via
  a reduction \emph{from} DQBF); it does not show \KFOL's $\setminus V$ is
  \emph{no more} expressive than DQBF, only that it is at least as hard.
  Because $\setminus V$ permits arbitrary interleaving and multiple rounds of
  hiding, unlike DQBF's fixed one-block-each shape, it is a live possibility
  --- not excluded by anything proved here --- that unrestricted \KFOL\
  forceability is harder than NEXPTIME, or even undecidable, matching
  Peterson--Reif--Azhar's own finding that the fully general (non-hierarchical)
  case can be undecidable. This is currently an unaddressed gap in this
  section, not merely an open conjecture, and it is more basic than either
  item below.
  \item \textbf{Is the hierarchical sub-fragment decidable at all?} Almost
  certainly yes, and probably by a routine argument: guess a Skolem-function
  table at each hierarchy tier and verify, the same proof shape that already
  shows plain DQBF is decidable (NEXPTIME-complete, not merely
  NEXPTIME-hard), applied tier-by-tier instead of in one block. We have not
  written this out, but we do not consider it a deep unknown.
  \item \textbf{Does the complexity match the Peterson--Reif--Azhar
  $(d{+}1)$-fold-exponential bound exactly, with a matching lower bound
  specific to \KFOL's syntax?} This is the genuinely open part: we have
  attempted no reduction in either direction, their hardness constructions
  are for their own abstract model and may or may not embed into \KFOL's
  prenex, terminal-tuple syntax, and there is no reason the bound has to
  transfer unchanged rather than shift with \KFOL-specific structure.
\end{enumerate}

This sharpens \Cref{subsec:coalitional-forceability}'s conjecture rather than
answering it: hierarchical $\setminus V$ is, by construction, exactly the
regime where visibility sets are pairwise comparable, and
\Cref{prop:cross-diagonal-forceability} already shows that a merely
\emph{comparable} crossing pattern is not enough to break the flattening
argument on its own (coupling was also required there). If the $K\ge 3$
separation conjecture is true, its witness must therefore use \emph{non}-hierarchical
$\setminus V$-annotations; testing whether coupled objectives plus
hierarchical (but nontrivial) independence already separates, before
attempting the harder non-hierarchical construction, is a natural
intermediate step we have not carried out.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Discussion}

Taken together, these results are more informative about \emph{where} $K$-role
structure matters than a bare list of hardness results would suggest:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The broadcast $K$-role fragment is PSPACE-complete, uniformly in $K$
        (\Cref{prop:pspace-core}). This uniformity is itself a signal: if
        $K>2$ roles carried strategic content beyond two-coalition
        alternation, we would expect some dependence of the complexity on
        $K$, and there is none.
  \item Coalitional forceability adds nothing further in the broadcast
        fragment: it reduces to ordinary two-role forceability of a merged
        game (\Cref{prop:coalition-merge-lossless}), and is PSPACE-complete
        for exactly that reason (\Cref{cor:coalitional-pspace}).
  \item Adding IF-style independence increases hardness to at least NEXPTIME
        (\Cref{prop:nexptime-if}), but the construction we give for this is
        itself a $K=2$, single-role phenomenon (\Cref{rem:nexptime-is-k2}).
\end{itemize}

So every hardness result in this section is, on inspection, a two-player or
single-role phenomenon stated in $K$-role notation. We think this is worth
saying plainly rather than leaving implicit: by themselves, these results show
that \KFOL\ forceability is at least as expressive as, and no harder than, the
two-player machinery it is built from --- they do not show that the $K$-role
structure is doing separate strategic work. Whether there is a genuinely
$K\ge 3$ phenomenon, in complexity or elsewhere in the calculus, is open; the
IF-binder coalition question above (and \Cref{sec:conclusion}) is where we
think it is most likely to be found.
