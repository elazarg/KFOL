\section{Owned choice}\label{sec:rational}

Throughout, $I$ is a finite set of $K\geq3$ \emph{roles}, or players; $a,b,c$ are distinct roles, and $r$ is a role acting as an owner. The case $K=2$ is different, and \cref{sec:discussion} returns to it. Our games are finite games of perfect information without chance moves, presented as binary trees: a leaf $\at i$ declares that $i$ wins, and a node $G\ch r H$ is
a public choice by its \emph{owner} $r$. A role values its own win at one and every other outcome at zero, so a role that cannot win has no opinion about who does. Ties are therefore everywhere, and we keep the winners of every equilibrium, however its ties are broken.

\begin{definition}[Equilibria and values]\label{game:def:spe}
A \emph{profile} of a game $G$ selects a branch at every node, including nodes that play never reaches, and its \emph{outcome} is the winner of the leaf it reaches from the root. A profile is a \emph{Nash equilibrium} if no role that loses under it can win by changing only its own choices, and a \emph{subgame-perfect equilibrium} (SPE) if it restricts to a Nash equilibrium of every subtree, reached or not. The \emph{value} $\den G$ is the set of outcomes of the SPEs of $G$.
\end{definition}

Choices that play never reaches can decide who wins. Consider
\[
 D=(\at a\ch b\at c)\ch a(\at b\ch b\at c).
\]
In \cref{fig:D}, on the right $b$ takes its own win, and on the left it loses whichever branch it picks. If it picks $c$ on the left, the root owner $a$ loses on both sides and may go right, so $b$ wins by a choice in a subtree that play never enters. Other SPEs are won by $a$ and by $c$, so $\den D=\{a,b,c\}$. Going right is weakly dominated for $a$, since only the left branch can make $a$ win. Refinements that exclude such choices, such as trembling-hand perfection \cite{Selten1975}, give $\{a,c\}$ here, but their outcome sets are not determined by those of subgames (\cref{app:proofs-rational}).

SPE values, in contrast, compose: the value of a choice depends only on the values of its branches, combined by \emph{owned choice}: for each role $r$ and all $S,T\subseteq I$,
\begin{equation}\label{game:eq:own}
 S\ow r T=\begin{cases}
 \{r\},&S=\{r\}\text{ or }T=\{r\},\\
 S\cup T,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Owned choice is union biased toward $\{r\}$: it is the join for the order in which $S\sqsubseteq_r T$ when $T=\{r\}$, or $S\subseteq T$ and $S\ne\{r\}$, just as union is the join for inclusion.

\begin{proposition}[Backward induction]\label{game:thm:adequacy}
Every game has an SPE, so values are nonempty. Moreover,
$\den{\at i}=\{i\}$ and $\den{G\ch r H}=\den G\ow r\den H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{idea}
If $r$ is the only equilibrium winner of a branch, every parent equilibrium is won by $r$, since $r$ can always enter that branch. Otherwise each branch has an equilibrium in which $r$ loses. Playing it in one branch lets $r$ rationally go to the other, whichever equilibrium is played there, so every equilibrium winner of either branch wins some SPE of the choice. In $D$ the branches have values $\{a,c\}$ and $\{b\}$, and $\den D=\{a,c\}\ow a\{b\}=\{a,b,c\}$.
\end{idea}

Call a singleton value an \emph{atom}, since singletons are the atoms of the lattice of sets, and a larger one \emph{ambiguous}. Atoms are values, not syntax: the leaf $\at i$ has the atom $\{i\}$ as its value. Induction on $G$ shows that $\den G=\{r\}$ exactly when $r$ can force a win in $G$, that is, has choices at its own nodes that win whatever the others choose. So an atom records a forced win, and an ambiguous value records that nobody can force one. Owned choice is thus choice by guarantee: the owner takes a branch where it can force a win if there is one, and otherwise neither branch guarantees it anything and all their equilibrium winners remain possible.

The argument for \cref{game:thm:adequacy} works unchanged for a choice among any finite nonempty set of branches: the owner wins alone if some branch has value $\{r\}$, and otherwise every equilibrium winner of every branch remains possible. This is the fold of $\ow r$ over the values of the branches, and since $\ow r$ is associative, commutative, and idempotent, a semilattice operation, the fold does not depend on how the branches are ordered or grouped. So a quantifier over a finite nonempty action set is a repeated binary choice. Infinite action sets add nothing when every play is finite: at each decision, at most $K-1$ well-chosen actions preserve the possible SPE winners, and the pruned game is finite (\cref{game:prop:choice-support}).

Terms extend games with variables and renamings:
\[
 t ::= \at i\mid x_j\mid t\ch r t\mid\pi(t),
 \qquad \pi\in\Sym(I),
\]
A variable stands for an unknown subgame, and $\pi(t)$ is $t$ with its roles renamed by $\pi$, so that its value is the image under $\pi$ of the value of $t$. Renaming generalizes negation, which two-player game semantics reads as swapping the players: the negations $\neg_{ij}$ of Olde Loohuis and Venema swap two roles \cite{OldeLoohuisVenema2010}, and these transpositions generate all renamings. A valuation assigns values to the variables, and a term then evaluates by \cref{game:thm:adequacy}. Substituting games for the variables gives a game whose value is the term evaluated at their values, since repeated occurrences of a variable are independently solved copies. An equation $s=t$ holds when both sides agree at every valuation, so that either side can replace the other in any game, whatever subgames fill the variables. These equations play the role that Boolean laws play for two-player evaluation games. With the values as carrier, the full signature gives the \emph{winner algebra} $\mathbf A^{\rm sym}$. Its reduct without renamings is $\mathbf A$, and its reduct to owned choices alone is the \emph{core} $\mathbf C$, whose terms have no winner leaves.

Role names serve only to match choices with the leaves they aim at. Renaming a whole game therefore changes nothing but names, while renaming a component changes how it matches its context. A role may also stand for a coalition: its choices may be split among agents that share its objective without changing any SPE, and a team that wins when any member does behaves as one role (\cref{app:proofs-rational,context:prop:team-refinement}). Coalitions must be fixed in advance, however: merging roles acts on values only in degenerate cases (\cref{olv:thm:coalition-maps}). With four roles, $\at b\ch a\at c$ and $\at b\ch d\at c$ both have value $\{b,c\}$, but if $a$ joins $b$ and $d$ joins $c$, the first is won by the team of $b$ and the second by the team of $c$.

Union is not among the operations, but three roles define it.

\begin{proposition}[Neutral union]\label{game:thm:union}
For pairwise distinct $r,s,t$,
\begin{equation}\label{game:eq:union}
 J(x,y)=(x\ow r y)\ow t(x\ow s y)=x\cup y.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

If just one inner choice returns its owner's atom, that atom belongs to the other result, and the outer owner is different; if both do, the outer choice unites them. We simply write $\cup$ from now on.

\begin{proposition}[Winner budget]\label{game:prop:winner-budget}
At every valuation, the value of a term has at most as many members as its leaves supply: one at each winner leaf, and the size of the variable's value at each occurrence of a variable. So the value of a game with $n$ choices has at most $n+1$ members. Every nonempty $S\subseteq I$ is the value of a game with exactly $|S|-1$ choices, and of no smaller game.
\end{proposition}

\begin{idea}
The value of a choice lies within the union of its branches' values, and renaming preserves sizes. For the construction, start from $\at a\ch c\at b$ with $a,b\in S$ and $c\notin\{a,b\}$, and add each further member of $S$ by a choice owned by $a$; the value is ambiguous and the new winner is not $a$, so each step is a union. The report counts the losses exactly (\cref{game:prop:winner-count,game:cor:ground-size}).
\end{idea}

With only two roles, ground terms would be Boolean: the two owned choices are join and meet once a role is chosen as truth. With three, every nonempty set of roles is the value of a game (\cref{game:prop:winner-budget}), and the algebra is not Boolean in any coordinates: it embeds in no product of Boolean algebras in which each owned choice acts coordinatewise as meet or join, as the choices of Olde Loohuis and Venema do (\cref{cor:no-boolean-product}). All such products satisfy the distributive law
\[
 (x\ow a y)\ow b z=(x\ow b z)\ow a(y\ow b z),
\]
and indifference breaks it. At $x=\{a\}$, $y=\{b\}$, $z=\{c\}$ the left side is $\{a\}\ow b\{c\}=\{a,c\}$, and the right side is the value $\{a,b,c\}$ of $D$.
