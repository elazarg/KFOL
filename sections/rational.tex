\section{Owned choice}\label{sec:rational}

Throughout, $I$ is a finite set of $K\geq3$ \emph{roles}, or players; $a,b,c$ are distinct roles, $r$ is a role acting as an owner, and $k$ is a role acting as an observer. The case $K=2$ is different, and \cref{sec:discussion} returns to it. We present games as finite binary trees: a leaf $\at i$ declares that $i$ wins, and a node $G\ch r H$ is
a public choice by its \emph{owner} $r$. A role values its own win at one and every other outcome at zero, so a role that cannot win has no opinion about who does. Ties are therefore everywhere, and we keep all of them.

\begin{definition}[Equilibria and values]\label{game:def:spe}
A \emph{profile} $\sigma$ of a game $G$ selects a branch at every node of $G$, including nodes that play never reaches. Its \emph{outcome} $\operatorname{out}(\sigma)$ is the winner of the leaf reached by following $\sigma$ from the root, and its \emph{restriction} $\sigma|_H$ to a subtree $H$ is the profile of $H$ that it contains. Write $\sigma'\sim_i\sigma$ when $\sigma'$ differs from $\sigma$ only at nodes owned by $i$. Then $\sigma$ is a \emph{Nash equilibrium} of $G$ if no losing role can win by changing only its own choices: for every role $i$,
\[
 \operatorname{out}(\sigma')=i\text{ for some }\sigma'\sim_i\sigma
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{out}(\sigma)=i.
\]
It is a \emph{subgame-perfect equilibrium} (SPE) of $G$ if $\sigma|_H$ is a Nash equilibrium of $H$ for every subtree $H$ of $G$, reached or not. The \emph{value} of $G$ is the set of its SPE outcomes,
\[
 \den G=\{\operatorname{out}(\sigma):\sigma\text{ is an SPE of }G\}.
\]
\end{definition}

Choices that play never reaches can decide who wins. Consider
\[
 D=(\at a\ch b\at c)\ch a(\at b\ch b\at c).
\]
On the right $b$ takes its own win, and on the left it loses whichever branch it picks. If it picks $c$ on the left, the root owner $a$ loses on both sides and may go right, so $b$ wins by a choice in a subtree that play never enters. Other SPEs are won by $a$ and by $c$, so $\den D=\{a,b,c\}$.

Backward induction finds values without examining whole profiles: the value of a choice depends only on the values of its branches, through an operation on the set $\Pow$ of nonempty subsets of $I$. For each role $r$, \emph{owned choice} by $r$ is the operation on $\Pow$ given by
\begin{equation}\label{game:eq:own}
 S\ow r T=\begin{cases}
 \{r\},&S=\{r\}\text{ or }T=\{r\},\\
 S\cup T,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Backward induction]\label{game:thm:adequacy}
Every game has an SPE, and
$\den{\at i}=\{i\}$, $\den{G\ch r H}=\den G\ow r\den H$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{idea}
If $r$ is the only equilibrium winner of a branch, every parent equilibrium is won by $r$, since $r$ can always enter that branch. Otherwise each branch has an equilibrium in which $r$ loses. Playing it in one branch lets $r$ rationally go to the other, whichever equilibrium is played there, so every equilibrium winner of either branch wins some SPE of the choice. In $D$ the branches have values $\{a,c\}$ and $\{b\}$, and $\den D=\{a,c\}\ow a\{b\}=\{a,b,c\}$.
\end{idea}

Call a singleton an \emph{atom} and a larger set \emph{ambiguous}. Induction on $G$ shows that $\den G=\{r\}$ exactly when $r$ can force a win in $G$, that is, has choices at its own nodes that win whatever the others choose. So an atom records a forced win, and an ambiguous value records that nobody can force one. Owned choice is thus choice by guarantee: the owner takes a branch where it can force a win if there is one, and otherwise neither branch guarantees it anything and all their equilibrium winners remain possible.

Terms extend games with variables and renamings:
\[
 t ::= \at i\mid x_j\mid t\ch r t\mid\pi(t),
 \qquad \pi\in\Sym(I),
\]
where $\pi(t)$ renames the roles of a component, so that its value is the image under $\pi$ of the value of $t$. A valuation assigns values to the variables, and a term then evaluates by \cref{game:thm:adequacy}; repeated occurrences of a variable are independently solved copies. We write $\mathbf A^{\rm sym}$ for this full signature, $\mathbf A$ for its reduct without permutations, and $\mathbf C$ for owned choices alone.

Each $\star_r$ is a semilattice operation, and the same argument makes a quantifier over a finite nonempty action set a fold of $\star_r$, so binary choice suffices. Infinite action sets add nothing when plays have bounded length: at each decision, at most $K-1$ well-chosen actions preserve the possible SPE winners (\cref{game:prop:choice-support}). With only two roles, ground terms would be Boolean: the two owned choices are join and meet once a role is chosen as truth. With three there is genuine ambiguity, and all of it is reachable.

\begin{proposition}[Neutral union]\label{game:thm:union}
For pairwise distinct $r,s,t$,
\begin{equation}\label{game:eq:union}
 J(x,y)=(x\ow r y)\ow t(x\ow s y)=x\cup y.
\end{equation}
Consequently every member of $\Pow$ is the value of a game.
\end{proposition}

If just one inner choice collapses, its owner's atom belongs to the other result, and the outer owner is different; if both collapse, the outer choice unites them. We simply write $\cup$ from now on.

The algebra is not Boolean in any coordinates. Indifference breaks distributivity: $\den{(\at a\ch a\at b)\ch b\at c}=\{a,c\}$, whereas distributing $\ch b$ over $\ch a$ gives $D$ (\cref{cor:no-boolean-product}).
