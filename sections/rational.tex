\section{Owned choice and rational play}
\label{sec:rational}

\subsection{Games, strategies, and equilibria}

Fix a finite set $I$ of $K\geq3$ \emph{roles}, which we also call players. A
game is a finite binary tree. A leaf $\at i$ declares that $i$ wins, and a node
$G\ch r H$ is a choice by its \emph{owner} $r$ between two continuations.
Everything is public. Preferences are as simple as they can be: a role values
its own win at one and every other outcome at zero, so a role that cannot win
has no opinion about who does. This indifference is not a simplification to be
removed later. It is the source of nearly everything in this paper: ties are
everywhere, and we keep all of them.

A \emph{full profile} prescribes an action at every internal node, including
nodes that the profile itself avoids. At a node it therefore consists of a root
action and a full profile for each child. A unilateral deviation by $i$ may
replace all the actions owned by $i$, in both child trees, and leaves every
other player's actions fixed. A profile is \emph{Nash} if no such deviation
improves the deviator's utility, and a \emph{subgame-perfect equilibrium} (SPE)
if its restriction to every subtree is Nash. We write $\B(G)$ for the set of
roles that win in some SPE of $G$.

Why insist on behavior at unreached nodes? Because that is what equilibria are
made of. Take
\[
 D=(\at0\ch1\at2)\ch0(\at1\ch1\at2).
\]
In the right subtree, owner $1$ must choose itself. In the left subtree, owner
$1$ loses either way and may choose either winner. The SPE profiles of $D$ are
therefore exactly these:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
Left child winner & Right child winner & Root action (owner $0$) & Winner\\
\midrule
$0$ & $1$ & left & $0$\\
$2$ & $1$ & left & $2$\\
$2$ & $1$ & right & $1$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The last row is the interesting one. Winner $1$ is possible only because of what
player $1$ is prepared to do in a subtree that is never entered: by prescribing
winner $2$ there, it leaves the root owner indifferent between two losing
choices. So $\B(D)=\{0,1,2\}$, and a semantics that recorded only behavior along
the path of play could not explain it.

Now fix a profile whose two child profiles are SPE, with child winners $x$ and
$y$. The parent profile is SPE exactly when it chooses a branch won by $r$
whenever $x=r$ or $y=r$; otherwise either branch is optimal for $r$. This
follows from whole-player deviations, since a deviation that wins in a child
would already improve that child's profile. It is not an assumption that
checking the current action alone suffices, which in games of partial
information would be false. Collecting the possibilities over all child
equilibria suggests the operation on nonempty sets $S,T\subseteq I$ that governs
the whole paper:
\begin{equation}\label{game:eq:own}
 S\ow r T =
 \begin{cases}
 \{r\},&S=\{r\}\text{ or }T=\{r\},\\
 S\cup T,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Exact public-tree interpretation]\label{game:thm:adequacy}
Every closed tree has a full pure SPE profile, and
\[
 \B(\at i)=\{i\},\qquad
 \B(G\ch r H)=\B(G)\ow r\B(H).
\]
Every element of the right-hand side has a full SPE witness on the left.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Existence follows inductively by choosing an owner-winning child whenever
one is available in the chosen child profiles. If one child has only
$r$-winning SPE profiles, every parent SPE must win for $r$, and one
exists. Otherwise each child has an SPE profile losing for $r$. For any
SPE winner $i$ in the left child, combine its witness with a losing-for-$r$
SPE in the right child and choose left. This is locally optimal, whether
or not $i=r$. The symmetric construction covers the right child.
Conversely, a parent SPE selects the winner of a child SPE. These facts
give both inclusions in~\eqref{game:eq:own}.
\end{proof}

This is backward induction for a very restricted utility model, and we do not
offer it as new game theory. Its role is to justify the finite algebra
$\Pow(I)$. \texttt{Basic.lean} defines full profiles, whole-player deviations,
Nash equilibrium, and SPE separately, and then proves the theorem as
\texttt{winners\_node}. Leaves carry only a winner label, with no strategic
terminal returns. The dependent interface of \cref{app:dependent} also permits
return records; a simultaneous decision at such a boundary lies outside this
perfect-information recurrence.

\subsection{The algebra of winner sets}

Call a singleton \emph{resolved} and a set with at least two members
\emph{ambiguous}. The full term language is
\[
 t ::= \at i\mid x_j\mid t\ch r t\mid\pi(t),
 \qquad i,r\in I,\quad \pi\in\Sym(I).
\]
The constant $\at i$ denotes $\{i\}$, the constructor $\ch r$ denotes $\star_r$,
and $\pi(t)$ internally permutes the roles of a component. An input variable is
evaluated in its original environment before the permutation is applied.
Substitution at repeated occurrences creates independent component copies; we
return to this point below.

We distinguish three signatures, and a few derived operations recur often enough
to name now.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Name & Definition & Used for\\
\midrule
$\mathbf A_I$ & $\star_r$ $(r\in I)$ and constants $\{i\}$ $(i\in I)$
  & the reduct; player views (\cref{sec:perspectives})\\
$\mathbf A_I^{\rm sym}$ & $\mathbf A_I$ and all internal role permutations
  & the full signature (\cref{sec:pair-tests})\\
$\mathbf C_I$ & $\star_r$ $(r\in I)$ alone, with variables
  & the intrinsic core (\cref{sec:intrinsic})\\
\midrule
$J(x,y)$ & $(x\ow r y)\ow t(x\ow s y)$, with $r,s,t$ distinct
  & neutral union $x\cup y$\\
$x\del y$ & $y$ if $y$ is a singleton, else $x\cup y$
  & reset (\cref{sec:reset})\\
$H_s(x,y,z)$ & $(y\star_s z)\del x$
  & guarded choice (\cref{sec:complexity,sec:one-owner})\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
All signatures have carrier $\Pow(I)$. External renaming of the roles is a
symmetry in every case; an internal permutation is an extra term constructor
only in the full signature.

Each $\star_r$ is associative, commutative, and idempotent. For associativity, a
three-input product is $\{r\}$ if any input is $\{r\}$, and is their union
otherwise; nonemptiness ensures that a union of inputs different from $\{r\}$
cannot become $\{r\}$. So a quantifier over a finite nonempty action set is simply
the fold of its owner's operation, and it adds no independent algebraic primitive.
Role permutations transport these operations and the leaf constants.

For two roles the ground-generated carrier has only the two singletons, and
$\star_1,\star_2$ are Boolean join and meet once a role is chosen as truth. This is
classical logic. With three roles there is genuine ambiguity, such as $\{0,1\}$, and
the following construction generates everything.

\begin{theorem}[Neutral union]\label{game:thm:union}
For pairwise distinct $r,s,t$,
\begin{equation}\label{game:eq:union}
 J(x,y)=(x\ow r y)\ow t(x\ow s y)=x\cup y.
\end{equation}
Consequently, if $K\geq3$, every nonempty subset of $I$ is the
denotation of a closed owned-choice tree.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If neither inner operation collapses, both return $x\cup y$. If just one
collapses, its owner singleton belongs to the other result, and the outer
owner is different. If both collapse, the inputs must be the two distinct
owner singletons; the outer operation returns their union. Folding $J$
over the leaf singletons realizes every nonempty subset.
\end{proof}

The definition uses three owned-choice nodes and two occurrences of each input.
Although it names three owners, its denotation is independent of the distinct
triple chosen. It is an equality of equilibrium-winner observations, not an
isomorphism of trees or strategy spaces. Ground realization is
\texttt{every\_winner\_set\_realized} in \texttt{Synthesis.lean}. The two occurrences cannot be avoided.

\begin{theorem}[Occurrence lower bound]\label{game:thm:lower}
Suppose $K\geq3$. In the syntax of owned binary choices and arbitrary
nonempty ground constants, any term that denotes $x\cup y$ for every
$x,y\in\Pow(I)$ contains at least two occurrences of $x$ and at least two
occurrences of $y$. Hence three binary nodes are optimal.
\end{theorem}

\begin{idea}
With a single occurrence of $x$, the term reads as a path from that occurrence to
the root. Union must vary with $x$ for every $y$, so no side value on the path can
ever equal its owner's singleton, and hence each step only adds winners. But the
innermost side value, at $y=\{r\}$, already contains some $k\ne r$, which cannot then
be removed, and this contradicts the required output $\{j,r\}$ at $x=\{j\}$. The
path obstruction is \texttt{no\_single\_use\_union} in Lean; the full argument is in
\cref{app:proofs-rational}.
\end{idea}

The cost measured here is \emph{syntactic occurrence}. A play enters just one of the
four component occurrences. The theorem establishes neither runtime duplication nor a
need for a contraction rule in a linear type system: additive branching may use a
context in both exclusive branches.

\subsection{Not Boolean, in any coordinates}

Indifference invalidates distributivity. For the game $D$ above we found three possible
winners, whereas
\[
 B((\at0\ch0\at1)\ch1\at2)=\{0,2\}.
\]
Role $0$ must choose winner $0$ in its subtree, and the root owner $1$ is then
indifferent between prescribed winners $0$ and $2$. The two trees differ exactly by
distributing $\ch1$ over $\ch0$. This is a real obstruction, and not an artifact of the
presentation.

\begin{corollary}[No Boolean meet/join product representation]
\label{cor:no-boolean-product}
For $K\ge3$, this generated algebra has no injective homomorphism into a
product of Boolean algebras in which each owned operation acts in each
coordinate as either meet or join.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Any pair of such coordinate operations satisfies
$(a\star_r b)\star_s c
 =(a\star_s c)\star_r(b\star_s c)$, whether the coordinate operations
coincide or differ. Products and subalgebras preserve this identity.
The displayed three-role calculation refutes it in the generated algebra.
An injective homomorphism would preserve and reflect the equality.
\end{proof}

\subsection{What repetition means}

A repeated variable stands for independently solved copies. That reading is forced by
the term algebra, but it is worth seeing what it excludes. Fixing one and the same
equilibrium in every copy of a component gives a different observation,
$U(f)(S)=\bigcup_{g\in S}f(\{g\})$, which agrees with the ordinary one for union but not
in general, and which is not preserved by substitution. A \emph{shared hidden} decision
is a different matter again: if one player's bit is hidden from the player who must guess it,
no pure equilibrium exists at all. These three readings of ``the same thing twice'' have
genuinely different meanings, and \cref{app:contexts} separates them.
