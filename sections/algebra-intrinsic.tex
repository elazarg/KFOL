\section{The intrinsic core}\label{sec:intrinsic}
\subsection{Idempotency identifies a smaller syntax}\label{gen:sec:core}

Which operations combine their inputs without adding anything of their own? A natural answer
is the \emph{idempotent} ones: a positive-arity term operation is idempotent when
$t(S,\ldots,S)=S$ for every nonempty $S$. This semantic condition turns out to determine the
syntax. For a variable occurrence, compose all role permutations on its path from the root, in
the order in which they act on its value.

\begin{theorem}[Exact idempotent elimination]\label{gen:thm:core}
A term is idempotent if and only if it has no winner leaves and every
variable occurrence has identity accumulated permutation. Consequently
the idempotent part of the full term clone is exactly the clone generated
by the $K$ binary owned choices, without constants or internal switches.
Recognition and extraction of such a term take polynomial time in the
explicit input description.
\end{theorem}

\begin{idea}
Work on the diagonal at ambiguous inputs. A winner leaf leaves a fixed nonempty constant in every
output there: the constant is transported by switches, survives unions, and a collapse only
replaces it by an owner singleton. But no role lies in all ambiguous sets. So an idempotent term
has no winner leaves, and without them every node on this cell is a union. The diagonal value is
then $\bigcup\pi S$ over all occurrences, and $\pi S\subseteq S$ for every pair forces $\pi$ to be
the identity. Conversely, $\pi(u\star_r v)=\pi u\star_{\pi(r)}\pi v$ pushes all switches to the
leaves, where they vanish. The full proof is in \cref{app:proofs-core}; the criterion and the
extraction are \path{FullTerm.idempotent_iff_clean} and \path{FullTerm.idempotent_extraction}.
\end{idea}

So the no-switching calculus contains \emph{every} idempotent definable operation. Internal
switching still enlarges the complete clone and changes its congruences, but it adds nothing here.
We did not choose this core for convenience; idempotency selects it. Two consequences follow.

\begin{corollary}[Semilattice trace]\label{gen:cor:trace}
On all-ambiguous inputs, every idempotent term is union over a fixed
nonempty subset of its arguments. These restrictions form exactly the
semilattice term clone, and restriction is a surjective clone
homomorphism. There is no near-unanimity term of any finite arity, and
no Mal'cev term. Every variable occurring in a core term is semantically
essential, so every valid core identity has the same variables on both
sides.
\end{corollary}

\begin{idea}
On ambiguous inputs a core term never collapses, so it is the union of its occurring variables.
Near-unanimity and Mal'cev identities imply idempotency, so any such term would lie in the core; but
a nonempty union cannot ignore one argument, as one sees by putting $I$ there and a pair elsewhere.
Regularity is the standard observation for algebras containing a two-element semilattice
\cite{Plonka1967}. See \cref{app:proofs-core}.
\end{idea}

\begin{corollary}[Intrinsic player views]\label{gen:cor:views}
Every idempotent operation in the full role-switching language acts
compositionally on each of the $K+1$-state player views
\[
 q_k(S)=
 \begin{cases}
 \pv,&k\in S,\\
 a_i,&S=\{i\},\ i\ne k,\\
 \nv,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\]
These give a subdirect representation by $K$ factors. The family is
irredundant: none of its player views can be omitted.
\end{corollary}

Each $q_k$ is a quotient for every owned choice by \cref{view:thm:view}, and \cref{gen:thm:core}
extends this to all idempotent operations, even when their original definitions contain internal
switches; this is \path{FullTerm.idempotent_sameView}. So the decomposition that fails for
unrestricted role switching holds intrinsically for all idempotent definable operations. The
representation is subdirect, not the full Cartesian product.

\subsubsection{An optimal logarithmic test}

How do we recognize idempotency semantically, without looking at the syntax? A fixed domain
$D\subseteq\Pow(I)$ \emph{certifies idempotency} when, for terms of every arity, agreement
$t(S,\ldots,S)=S$ for $S\in D$ implies idempotency. This tests a specific diagonal law, rather than
all term equations, and it needs very few values.

\begin{theorem}[Exact ambiguous certificates]\label{gen:thm:idem-domain}
For a domain consisting of ambiguous values, certification holds exactly
when
\[
 \bigcap D=\varnothing,\qquad
 (\mathbf1_{i\in S})_{S\in D}\text{ are distinct for all }i\in I.
\]
The minimum size of a certifying domain, even allowing singleton test
values, is three for $K=3$, and $\lceil\log_2(K+1)\rceil$ for $K\geq4$.
Both minima are attained using ambiguous values alone.
\end{theorem}

\begin{idea}
The two conditions rule out the only ways to fool the test: a constant present in every test, and a
permutation exchanging two roles that no test tells apart. Conversely, these are the only obstacles,
by \cref{gen:thm:core}. Assigning each role a binary word recording which tests contain it reduces
the size question to counting words, with the all-one word excluded. See \cref{app:proofs-core}.
\end{idea}

At $K=7$, three ambiguous diagonal tests suffice, compared with $28$ values for testing every
full-signature equation. The improvement is specific to idempotency.

\subsection{One common source}\label{gen:sec:sep}

Our next theorem is the simplest structural constraint we know on what terms can do. Fix input
environments $v_a$ and pairwise disjoint target regions $W_a\subseteq I$. A \emph{source occurrence}
is a winner leaf, or a variable leaf together with its accumulated permutation.

\begin{theorem}[One common source]\label{gen:thm:source}
If $t(v_a)\subseteq W_a$ for every $a$, one source occurrence of $t$
already has its value inside $W_a$ in every environment simultaneously.
If there are at least two target regions, it is a variable occurrence.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At an owned node with owner $r$, at most one target contains $r$.
In every other environment the output cannot collapse to $\{r\}$;
it is union, so both children lie in the target. In the possible
exceptional environment, either both children lie in the target or a
child is the absorbing owner singleton and lies in it. Choose that child:
it works in all environments. Descend to a leaf. At a switch pull the
targets back along the permutation, preserving disjointness. A winner
leaf has the same nonempty value in all environments and cannot fit
two disjoint targets.
\end{proof}

Distinct singleton outputs at any number of tests are thus jointly explained by one occurrence, which
cannot change between tests. \texttt{Separation.lean} proves this for arbitrary index types and target
predicates, with internal permutations explicit in the syntax.

The theorem tells us what determinism costs. Call a term \emph{atom-preserving} if it returns a
singleton on every singleton-input valuation. It induces a function $f:I^n\to I$.

\begin{theorem}[Exact deterministic atomic clone]\label{gen:thm:S2}
At every positive arity, the functions induced by atom-preserving terms
are exactly the essentially unary or at most two-valued functions.
This is the known clone $S_2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{idea}
Let $\iota_3$ be the relation of triples with a repeated entry. If three environments have this property
in every coordinate and yet produced three distinct singleton outputs, \cref{gen:thm:source} would give
one permutation sending a triple with a repeat to three distinct roles. So induced functions preserve
$\iota_3$, and the established presentation $\operatorname{Pol}(\iota_3)=S_2$ \cite{Szendrei2012} gives
the upper bound. Conversely, $x\star_a(a\ b)(x)$ merges atomic $b$ into $a$, which yields every unary map;
and $\star_a,\star_b$ act as OR and AND on $\{a\},\{b\}$, which yields every two-valued function. See
\cref{app:proofs-core}.
\end{idea}

\begin{corollary}[Idempotent atomic determinacy]\label{gen:cor:determinacy}
An idempotent, atom-preserving term projects one input on all atomic
valuations. A genuinely multiargument rule using at least three output
roles must return ambiguity somewhere on atomic inputs.
\end{corollary}

Indeed, idempotency makes the atomic function surjective, so as $K\geq3$ it must be essentially unary, and
the diagonal law makes that unary map the identity. In other words, an idempotent rule built from rational play has no deterministic way of
aggregating unique-winner inputs, beyond copying one of them.

The exact number of induced $n$-ary atomic functions is
\[
 \binom K2(2^{K^n}-2)+K+
 n\left(K^K-\binom K2(2^K-2)-K\right).
\]
The first part counts functions with at most two values, and the remaining essentially unary functions have
a unique essential argument. For $K=3,n=2$ the count is $1545$. This classifies deterministic atomic behavior;
mixed and ambiguous valuations remain available for richer operations.
