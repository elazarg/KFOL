\section{The intrinsic core}\label{sec:intrinsic}

Which operations combine their inputs without adding anything of their own? Call a term operation \emph{idempotent} if $t(S,\ldots,S)=S$ for every $S$. This semantic condition determines the syntax. For a variable occurrence, compose the role permutations on its path from the root.

\begin{theorem}[Idempotent elimination]\label{gen:thm:core}
A term is idempotent if and only if it has no winner leaves and every variable occurrence has identity accumulated permutation. Consequently the idempotent term operations are exactly those of $\mathbf C$, and an equivalent term can be extracted in polynomial time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{idea}
Evaluate the diagonal on ambiguous inputs. A winner leaf leaves a fixed nonempty constant in every output, but no role lies in all ambiguous sets. Without winner leaves every node is a union, so the value is $\bigcup\pi S$, and $\pi S\subseteq S$ for every pair forces $\pi$ to be the identity.
Conversely, $\pi(u\star_r v)=\pi u\star_{\pi(r)}\pi v$ pushes switches to the leaves, where they vanish.
\end{idea}

So idempotency singles out $\mathbf C$. On ambiguous inputs a core term is the union of its variables, which excludes near-unanimity and Mal'cev terms and means that identities between core terms have the same variables on both sides (\cref{gen:cor:trace}), and the player views apply to all idempotent operations, even those defined with internal
switches (\cref{gen:cor:views}).

The next theorem is the simplest constraint we know on what terms can do. A \emph{source occurrence} is a winner or variable leaf together with its accumulated permutation.

\begin{theorem}[One common source]\label{gen:thm:source}
Fix valuations $v_e$ and pairwise disjoint regions $W_e\subseteq I$. If $t(v_e)\subseteq W_e$ for every $e$, then one source occurrence of $t$ has its value inside $W_e$ in every valuation simultaneously. With at least two regions, it is a variable occurrence.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At an owned node with owner $r$, at most one target contains $r$.
In every other valuation the output cannot collapse to $\{r\}$;
it is union, so both children lie in the target. In the possible
exceptional valuation, either both children lie in the target or a
child is the absorbing owner atom and lies in it. Choose that child:
it works in all valuations. Descend to a leaf. At a switch pull the
targets back along the permutation, preserving disjointness. A winner
leaf has the same nonempty value in all valuations and cannot fit
two disjoint targets.
\end{proof}

So three valuations with a repeated entry in every input cannot produce three distinct atoms, since \cref{gen:thm:source} would give one permutation doing so. In particular a term has no if-then-else: none returns $y$ when $x=\{a\}$ and $z$ when $x=\{b\}$ for all atoms $y,z$, as the atomic inputs $(x,y,z)=(a,a,a),(a,b,a),(b,a,c)$ show.

The same fact shows what determinism costs. Call a term \emph{atom-preserving} if it returns an atom on atomic inputs, so that it induces a function $f:I^n\to I$. Then $f$ preserves the relation of triples with a repeat, and by the known classification of that clone \cite{Szendrei2012}, $f$ is essentially unary or takes at most two values (\cref{gen:thm:S2}). An idempotent $f$ is surjective, and the diagonal law then gives the following.

\begin{corollary}[Determinacy]\label{gen:cor:determinacy}
An idempotent atom-preserving term projects one input on atomic valuations.
\end{corollary}
