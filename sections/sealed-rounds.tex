\section{Sealed rounds}\label{sec:sealed-rounds}

What happens when a move can be hidden? Suppose one player chooses a bit and another guesses it. With public moves the guesser wins. Hide the bit, and at any pair of pure actions one of them wishes to switch, so there is no pure equilibrium. Values must therefore retain probabilities.

There is a clean case in which they compose: rounds in which everything is disclosed at the end. The games are now leaves $\at i$ and rounds. A round $\mathsf R_A(\alpha\mapsto G_\alpha)$, with $A=\prod_{i\in I}A_i$ for finite nonempty action sets $A_i$, works as follows. Each role commits privately, all actions are then revealed, and play continues with the public continuation $G_\alpha$. A role with more than one action is \emph{active}: one active role gives public owned choice, and two active roles with Boolean menus give a sealed two-bit round. We can read a round as a multistage game with observed actions, or implement it serially, with the roles committing one at a time and unseen. Let $\Delta$ be the simplex of distributions on $I$, and let $\mathcal E(G)\subseteq\Delta$ be the set of winner distributions of behavioral sequential equilibria (SE) of the serial implementation of $G$; their coordinates are the players' expected utilities. For nonempty sets $P_\alpha\subseteq\Delta$, define
\begin{equation}\label{se:eq:round}
 \mathcal N_A((P_\alpha)_\alpha)=
 \left\{\sum_{\alpha\in A}\left(\prod_i\sigma_i(\alpha_i)\right)p_\alpha:
 \begin{array}{l}
 p_\alpha\in P_\alpha\text{ for every }\alpha,\\
 \sigma\text{ is a mixed Nash equilibrium of the finite game}\\
 \hfill u_i(\alpha)=(p_\alpha)_i
 \end{array}\right\}.
\end{equation}
A value $p_\alpha$ is selected for every tuple, including those of probability zero, and this records the off-path continuation.

\begin{proposition}[Round recursion]\label{se:thm:round}
Mixed SPE winner distributions of the observed-action game coincide with SE winner distributions of its serial commitment implementation. For these common values,
\[
 \mathcal E(\at i)=\{\delta_i\},\qquad
 \mathcal E(\mathsf R_A(\alpha\mapsto G_\alpha))=
        \mathcal N_A((\mathcal E(G_\alpha))_\alpha).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{idea}
The mixed-SPE recursion is the standard one-stage-deviation argument \cite{FudenbergTirole1991}. For the serial implementation, perturb every action law toward uniform. At a public history the reach factor cancels from Bayes' rule, so the belief over earlier commitments is the product of the prescribed laws, even off the path. Beliefs thus carry nothing
beyond the strategy.
\end{idea}

On public trees, mixing adds no possible winner (\cref{se:cor:public}), so the winner-set algebra is exactly right for public games. Its laws belong to that observation, however, and not to the distributions: for $T=(\at a\ch c\at b)\ch a\at c$, the distribution $(\delta_a+\delta_c)/2$ lies in $\mathcal E(T\ch b T)$ but not in $\mathcal E(T)$ (\cref{se:eq:nonconvex}), so public choice is not idempotent on distribution sets and the earlier rewrites do not carry over. Sealing goes further. With two active roles and every leaf won by one of them, a game is constant-sum, and its value is $a$'s winning probability $v(G)$. Consider the round $D(G,H)$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
 & $b:0$ & $b:1$\\\hline
$a:0$ & $G$ & $\at b$\\
$a:1$ & $\at b$ & $H$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Player $a$ wants to match and $b$ to mismatch. Write $x=v(G)$ and $y=v(H)$. If $x+y>0$, either player, by playing $0$ with probability $y/(x+y)$, fixes $a$'s winning probability at $xy/(x+y)$, so $v(D(G,H))=xy/(x+y)$: the parallel sum of network theory, and the classical value of a diagonal matrix game \cite{AndersonDuffin1969,FergusonNotes}. If $x=y=0$ the value is $0$. So strategy alone produces new probabilities.

Put $L_0=\at a$ and $L_{n+1}=D(L_n,\at a)$. Then $v(L_n)=1/(n+1)$. For $n\geq0$ let $G_n=L_{n+1}$, so every $G_n$ has possible winners $\{a,b\}$. The winner-set observation cannot tell these games apart, but a third player can. Let $R_m$ be $G_m$ with $c$ in place of $b$, and consider the public context $F_m[-]=[-]\ch a R_m$.

\begin{theorem}[No finite winner algebra]\label{se:thm:infinite}
For all $n,m\geq0$,
\[
 \operatorname{Win}(\mathcal E(F_m[G_n]))=
 \begin{cases}
 \{a,b\},&n<m,\\
 \{a,b,c\},&n=m,\\
 \{a,c\},&n>m,
 \end{cases}
\]
where $\operatorname{Win}(P)$ is the set of roles with positive probability in some member of $P$. So no finite-valued compositional semantics of the round language can preserve even player $c$'s possible-win observation, already with three roles and Boolean commitments.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The root owner compares $1/(n+2)$ with $1/(m+2)$, selecting the larger value, or either continuation on equality. This gives the formula. Player $c$ is thus a possible winner exactly when $m\leq n$, so distinct indices respond differently to the contexts $F_m$, and an adequate compositional semantics must assign them distinct values.
\end{proof}

In fact, on the games built from $\at a$ and $\at b$ by $D$, $\ch a$, $\ch b$, and exchanging $a$ with $b$, possible winners see every difference of value (\cref{se:cor:scalar}). A secret retained across a boundary is another matter: the interface must then carry joint conditional laws and consistent beliefs, and we have no composition theorem for that case.
